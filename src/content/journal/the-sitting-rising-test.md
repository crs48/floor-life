Few everyday movements have received quite so much dramatic publicity as sitting down and getting up. One minute you are beside the coffee table. The next, the internet is asking you to calculate your remaining years.

Let’s put the teacup down and separate the useful test from the alarming headline.

## What researchers measured

The Sitting–Rising Test records the supports and balance adjustments someone uses to lower to the floor and return to standing. Strength, flexibility, coordination, body proportions, health, and familiarity can all affect the task. It is not a test of whether you are a good person at furniture.

In the original cohort, 2,002 adults aged 51–80 performed the test and were followed for mortality. Lower scores were associated with higher all-cause mortality after adjustment for age, sex, and body mass index. **Researchers observed a relationship; they did not assign people a floor-living habit.** [Original study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23242910/).

The later study followed 4,282 adults aged 46–75 and again linked lower scores with higher natural and cardiovascular mortality. This extends the association in a clinical cohort, rather than proving that rehearsing the test prevents death. [Later study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40569873/).

## Why a group pattern is not your prognosis

A person with painful knees might need a hand because of the knees. Someone unfamiliar with the movement might discover a better strategy on a second attempt. A person with excellent balance might still have a health condition the test cannot detect.

A cohort result describes a pattern across people. Applying its average risk to one reader, without their clinical context, jumps over the most important part of the reasoning. Statistical adjustment helps address measured differences; it cannot remove every possible explanation.

It is also possible to get better at a test through practice without changing everything that produced its association with health. A higher score is not a receipt for extra years.

> Use the information to ask a useful question, not to pass judgment on your future.

## How the scoring works

Sitting and rising each start with five points. Each support subtracts one, and unsteadiness subtracts a half point in the affected phase. The phase scores are added. A hand counts in the scoring system; in real life it may simply be the sensible way to get up.

There is an interactive example on the evidence page if you want to understand the arithmetic. There is no need to try the physical test to read this article.

## A better question than “Did I get ten?”

Try: **“Would I like getting down and back up to feel easier?”** That is a practical goal you can discuss with a physiotherapist or another qualified clinician.

Useful things to notice include whether the movement hurts, whether you feel steady, whether you can find a reliable support, and whether you have a way to return to a seat. New difficulty or persistent symptoms deserve attention; an online score cannot explain their cause.

For unfamiliar transfers, work with a helper or professional. Keep a stable chair available and use your hands. [NHS guidance describes supported routes](https://www.guysandstthomas.nhs.uk/health-information/falls/what-do-if-you-fall), with the important distinction that after an actual fall you first check for injury and seek help when needed.

## Your next move can be small

Maybe it is practising a comfortable transfer with guidance. Maybe it is strengthening at chair height. Maybe today it is choosing a cushion that makes reading pleasant.

The goal is more useful options in daily life. If the test helps start that conversation, lovely. If the number makes you anxious, you can leave the number on the floor.
